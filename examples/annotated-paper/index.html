# Page 1 — the claim ! With 23 people, a shared birthday is already more likely than not, because what grows is the number of **pairs**. (1) A short note: two authors, one result, no new theory. Good for learning *how* to read a paper. (2) The abstract gives the whole story in four moves: question → surprising answer (23) → why (pairs grow like n²) → where it matters (hashes). ex: if you only read one paragraph of a paper, read the abstract and check you can say each move in your own words. (3) The key intuition: 23 people make 23 × 22 / 2 = **253 pairs**. Each pair is a chance of a match. - We think “me vs the others” (22 chances); the paper asks you to think “anyone vs anyone” (253 chances). (4) The approximation, eq. (2). f: p(n) ≈ 1 − exp(−n² / 730) - 730 = 2 × 365: the n² comes from counting pairs, n(n − 1)/2. (5) Solving p = 1/2 gives n ≈ √(730 · ln 2) ≈ 22.5 → **23**. ?? Why ln 2? Because exp(−x) = 1/2 exactly when x = ln 2 ≈ 0.693. → Bottom of page 1: the exact formula and the table.
# The exact derivation ! Compute the opposite — “everyone different” — and subtract it from 1. (1) The trick of the complement: “at least one match” has many cases; “no match at all” has only one, and it is easy to count. - Assumptions: 365 equally likely days, independent people, no 29 February. (2) Equation (1): person 2 must avoid 1 day, person 3 must avoid 2 days, … f: q(n) = 365/365 · 364/365 · … · (365 − n + 1)/365, p(n) = 1 − q(n) warn: in the PDF the equation wraps onto two lines — it is a single product. (3) Table 1, to memorise three anchors: | n | p(n) | |---|---| | 23 | 0.507 | | 50 | 0.970 | | 70 | 0.999 | - Already at 50 people a match is practically certain. → Page 2: the figure, the simulation and hashes.
# Page 2 — checking and using the result ! The approximation is good enough to reason with, and the same √ rule appears every time collisions matter. (1) Figure 1: solid = exact, dashed = approximation. They are almost on top of each other. - The grey cross marks the famous point: p = 0.5 at n = 23. (2) The simulation is the sanity check: 100,000 random groups of 23 → 0.510, close to 0.507. - Real birthdays are not uniform, which only makes matches **more** likely. (3) Why computer scientists care: with d possible hash values, collisions appear after ≈ √d items. ex: a 64-bit hash → about 2^{32} ≈ 4 × 10^{9} items before a collision is likely. > Mitzenmacher & Upfal (2005) treat this “birthday bound” in their chapter on balls and bins. (4) The one-line lesson: when every pair can trigger an event, chances grow with n², not n. → Done: try pressing N to compare the clean page with this sheet.
