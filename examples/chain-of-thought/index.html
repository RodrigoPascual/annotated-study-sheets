# Page 1 — what the paper claims ! Show a large model a few worked examples that **write out their reasoning**, and it starts reasoning step by step on new problems. (1) The title in one line: a prompt (no training) *elicits* reasoning — the ability is already in the model; the prompt draws it out. (2) Google Research, Brain Team. Published at NeurIPS 2022 (footer of this page). (3) The definition to remember: a chain of thought = a series of ==intermediate reasoning steps== in natural language, written before the answer. - chain-of-thought prompting = put a few such demonstrations (*exemplars*) in the prompt. - "emerge naturally in sufficiently large" models → scale matters (Figure 4). (4) Headline: PaLM 540B + just **eight** exemplars → state of the art on GSM8K, beating even finetuned GPT-3 with a verifier. ?? Three models or five? The abstract says three; §3.1 lists five (GPT-3, LaMDA, PaLM, UL2, Codex). The main figures use LaMDA, GPT and PaLM. → Figure 1, lower on this page: the method in one picture.

# Introduction and Figure 2 ! Making models bigger was not enough for multi-step reasoning; a better prompt was. (1) Scaling up brought better performance and sample efficiency, but "has not proved sufficient" for arithmetic, commonsense and symbolic reasoning (Rae et al., 2021). (2) Figure 2. y = solve rate: % of GSM8K math word problems answered correctly. | System | Solve rate (%) | |---|---| | Finetuned GPT-3 175B | 33 | | Prior best | 55 | | PaLM 540B, standard prompting | 18 | | PaLM 540B, chain of thought | **57** | - Hatched bars: finetuned results from Cobbe et al. (2021). Solid bars: the same PaLM, prompting only. - 18 → 57 with no training at all (my arithmetic: > 3×). (3) Two ideas combined: natural-language **rationales** help arithmetic (but needed training on them) + **few-shot prompting** (no training, a few input–output exemplars). → Next: why each idea alone falls short.
# The gap and the fix ! Prompt with triples ⟨input, chain of thought, output⟩: the benefit of rationales without any training. (1) Why each idea alone fails: - rationale-augmented training needs a large set of high-quality rationales — costly to write; - standard few-shot prompting works poorly on reasoning and often barely improves with scale. - The fix: every exemplar = question + reasoning steps + answer. (2) Why prompting-only matters: no large training dataset, and one model checkpoint can do many tasks. - Evaluated on arithmetic, commonsense and symbolic reasoning. (3) §2 starts from how people solve problems: "she has 10 … then … 7 … so the answer is 7" — decompose, solve each step, then answer. ?? Isn't that just an explanation? They avoid that word: explanations usually come *after* the answer; a chain of thought comes *before* it and leads to it (p. 3). → Page 3: four properties.
# Four reasons it is attractive ! A chain of thought gives the model room to compute and gives us a window to inspect its reasoning. (1) Why the name: it reads like a solution, but it is produced *before* the answer and mimics step-by-step thinking. (2) Decomposition: multi-step problems become intermediate steps, so harder problems get more computation (more generated tokens). (3) Interpretability: see *how* the answer was reached and debug where the reasoning went wrong. warn: the paper itself says fully characterising the model's computations "remains an open question": the written chain is not guaranteed to be what the model computed. (4) Generality: math, commonsense, symbolic manipulation — in principle any task humans solve via language. (5) Cheap: off-the-shelf large models, just add exemplars to the prompt. ?? Is (2), extra computation, why it works? The ablation (last slide) says no: dots instead of words do not help. → Section 3: the experiments.
# §3.1 Experimental setup ! Eight hand-written exemplars, five math benchmarks, standard prompting vs chain of thought. (1) Arithmetic first: simple for humans, hard for language models. Claim: 540B + chain of thought ≈ task-specific finetuned models, and new state of the art on GSM8K. (2) Five math word-problem benchmarks: - **GSM8K** (Cobbe et al., 2021) — multi-step, the hardest here; - **SVAMP** (varying structures), **ASDiv** (diverse), **AQuA** (algebraic, multiple choice), **MAWPS**. (3) Baseline = standard prompting (Brown et al., 2020): Q/A exemplars, the model answers directly (Figure 1, left). (4) Proposed: the same exemplars, each with a chain of thought added. Eight written by hand because most datasets only have an evaluation split; full set in Appendix Table 20. warn: the exemplars "did not undergo prompt engineering"; robustness to other writers is tested in §3.4. → Page 4: what such exemplars look like across tasks.

# Models and the first result ! Chain of thought is an **emergent** ability: it helps only at around 100B parameters. (1) The same 8 exemplars for every math benchmark except AQuA (multiple choice): 4 exemplars from its training set. (2) Five model families, several sizes each: | Model | Sizes | |---|---| | GPT-3 (InstructGPT) | 350M, 1.3B, 6.7B, 175B | | LaMDA | 422M, 2B, 8B, 68B, 137B | | PaLM | 8B, 62B, 540B | | UL2 · Codex | 20B · code-davinci-002 | - Greedy decoding. LaMDA: averaged over 5 random seeds (exemplar orders). (3) Takeaway 1: no gain for small models; gains only with models of ∼100B parameters. warn: small models wrote fluent but illogical chains and did **worse** than standard prompting. Not a trick for small models. → Page 5: Figure 4, the scaling curves.

# §3.3 Ablation — what actually helps? ! Not the equation, not extra tokens, not reasoning after the fact: the natural-language steps **before** the answer. An *ablation* swaps out one ingredient at a time to see which one causes the effect. (1) Figure 5: GSM8K solve rate, LaMDA 137B and PaLM 540B, five prompt variants. Only the solid orange bar (chain of thought) stands out. - Equation only (p. 5): barely helps on GSM8K, whose questions are too hard to turn directly into an equation; it does help on one- or two-step datasets. (2) Variable compute only: output dots (…) as long as the equation → about the same as the baseline. More tokens alone is not it. (3) Chain of thought after the answer → about the same as the baseline. The reasoning must come first. > Wei, J. et al. (2022).
